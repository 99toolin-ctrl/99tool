What is the Infinite Power (P=VI) Solver?

The Infinite Power Solver is an electrical auditing calculator designed to resolve the relationships between Power ($P$), Voltage ($V$), and Current ($I$). In electrical physics, this relationship is established by the primary equation $P = V \times I$. It determines the electric power (measured in Watts) generated or consumed when a given current flows through an electrical potential difference.

This utility enables you to enter any two values to solve for the remaining metric instantly. It helps you design electrical circuits, size components, and check appliance power requirements without performing manual algebraic manipulations.

How to Use the Power Solver

You can calculate the missing value by inputting any two variables:

  1. Identify the two known parameters from your electrical specifications (Power, Voltage, or Current).
  2. Enter these values into their corresponding input fields:
    • Power in Watts (W)
    • Voltage in Volts (V)
    • Current in Amps (A)
  3. Leave the third input field completely empty.
  4. Click the "Solve Missing Value" button to perform the calculation.
  5. Observe the computed value automatically populated in the previously empty input box, and check the solved formula details below.
  6. Click "Clear" to wipe all values and prepare for another load check.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between Watts, Volts, and Amps?
Watts measure the total rate of energy transfer (Power). Volts measure the electrical pressure (Voltage). Amps measure the rate of charge flow (Current).

How does AC phase angle affect this calculation?
This calculator solves for DC circuits or pure resistive AC loads (where the power factor is 1.0). For inductive AC loads (like motors), true power calculations require incorporating the power factor ($P = V \times I \times PF$).

What is standard household voltage?
Standard household mains voltage is typically $120\text{ V}$ in North America and $230\text{ V}$ in Europe, Asia, and most other regions.

Why is my solved current higher than my breaker limit?
If the calculated current exceeds your circuit breaker's rating (usually 15A or 20A for household outlets), running the appliance will trip the breaker.
